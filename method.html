Theory, hypotheses, tests, sources, limitations and reproducibility

Theory: the core–periphery model with mobile graduates

The project uses Krugman’s (1991) core–periphery model (Lectures 7–8) in the “footloose entrepreneur” version of Forslid and Ottaviano (2003):

  • Graduates and knowledge workers are mobile. They move to wherever their career prospects are better. Each firm needs skilled people, so firms move with them.
  • Manufacturing workers with skills specific to one local industry, and the less mobile population, stay put. Their spending keeps demand in the periphery.
  • Mechanism: when trading between regions gets cheaper, a small advantage grows through circular cumulative causation (more graduates → more firms → more varied jobs → more graduates). Past the break point, skilled workers and their firms concentrate in one region.
  • Extensions used: housing costs in the core as a force pushing activity out (Helpman, 1998); Martin’s (1999) critique that the model ignores institutions and firm structure.

Hypotheses

Hypothesis Key prediction
A Milan’s pull (inside the model) Graduates and knowledge jobs concentrate in Milan faster than people; other cities lose share
B Industry decline (a shock from outside the model) Turin’s and Genoa’s losses come from the industries they had; specialised cities grow less
C Shock, then amplification Industry losses first; then local decline and graduate outflow speed up

The predictions were written down before looking at the data. Full assumptions and predictions: framework.md.

T5 Shift-share (national, industry-mix and local effects), 2008–14 and 2014–23, reference Italy, 11 sectors Part 2
T6 Manufacturing location quotient in 2008 vs job growth 2008–23, 47 provinces Part 2

Shift-share is a standard method in regional economics (Dunn, 1960). The location quotient (Lectures 9–10) is a province’s share of jobs in manufacturing divided by Italy’s share.

Main choices:

  • “Northern Italy” = the 47 provinces of the North-West and North-East.
  • Jobs and GDP use only 2008–2023, because the provincial job series has a methodological break in 2008.
  • Knowledge jobs = ICT + finance + professional and administrative services.
  • Census comparisons use shares only, with Milan and Monza combined.

Full details: sources.md.

Limitations

  • The car industry is hidden inside “manufacturing”, so hypothesis B is inferred for Turin rather than measured.
  • Jobs data starts only in 2008.
  • Jobs are counted at the workplace and graduates at residence, so commuting into Milan blurs the comparison.
  • Turin’s finance jobs almost double between 2017 and 2023 for reasons we could not establish (possibly a change in where a large bank registers its staff). Results that depend on finance are reported with and without it.
  • Graduate migration data covers only 2019–2023, so Turin’s net gain of graduates is a recent snapshot.
  • The methods are descriptive: they show patterns consistent with or against each hypothesis, not proof of cause.

Next step. The natural extension is to test the car industry directly, using ISTAT data on detailed sectors, so that hypothesis B for Turin is measured rather than inferred.

Reproduce it

Everything is on the my-supervision branch: the Python scripts in code/ download every dataset from the official sources and produce all tables, figures and the data used by this website. The steps, in order, are in analysis.md.

The AI wrote and ran the code, explained the methods and checked my numbers. Every step, including what I asked, what it contributed and what I checked, is recorded in workflow.md.
