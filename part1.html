1. Is the North becoming a single core?

Testing hypothesis A: is Milan pulling in graduates, knowledge jobs and the rest of the North?

What the model predicts. In the footloose-entrepreneur version of the core–periphery model, graduates are the mobile factor and firms follow them. If the North is tipping into a single core, Milan’s share of graduates and knowledge jobs should rise faster than its share of people, and the other cities should lose ground.

What we test, and what each hypothesis predicts

Each row is one test. The three middle columns say what we would expect to see if each hypothesis were true; the last column says what the data shows. Each test has its own section below, with an interactive chart.

TipWhat this shows · T1 and T4

Milan’s share of northern jobs rose from 14.2% to 15.9% between 2008 and 2023. That is about twice the relative increase in its population share (11.2% to 11.8%), and Milan beat national trends in almost every sector. → T1 supports A.

But Bologna, Verona and Padua all increased their share of northern jobs (+5% to +9%) and knowledge jobs (+7% to +13%). Milan grows alongside other cities rather than at their expense. → T4 is against A: the North looks polycentric, not like a single core.

P4 · Are knowledge jobs concentrating in Milan?

The home market ratio is a city’s share of northern knowledge jobs divided by its share of northern population. Above 1, the city has more than its “fair share” of knowledge firms; a rising ratio would mean concentration is increasing, as the model predicts for the core.

viewof shownHM = Inputs.checkbox(cities, {label: "Cities", value: ["Milan", "Turin", "Genoa", "Bologna"]})
viewof noFinanceHM = Inputs.toggle({label: "Exclude finance", value: false})
hmData = {
  const k = noFinanceHM ? "Knowledge jobs (without finance)" : "Knowledge jobs";
  const pop = new Map(shares.filter(d => d.measure === "Population").map(d => [d.province + d.year, d.share]));
  return shares.filter(d => d.measure === k && shownHM.includes(d.province))
    .map(d => ({province: d.province, year: d.year, ratio: d.share / pop.get(d.province + d.year)}));
}
Plot.plot({
  width,  // the page width, so text stays readable on phones
  height: 360, marginRight: 110,
  x: {label: null, tickFormat: "d"},
  y: {label: "share of knowledge jobs ÷ share of population", grid: true, zero: true},
  color: {...colourFor(shownHM), legend: true},
  marks: [
    Plot.ruleY([1], {strokeOpacity: 0.5, strokeDasharray: "4 3"}),
    Plot.lineY(hmData, {x: "year", y: "ratio", stroke: "province", strokeWidth: 2,
      tip: {format: {x: "d", y: ".2f", stroke: true}}}),
    Plot.text(hmData, Plot.selectLast({x: "year", y: "ratio", z: "province", text: "province",
      textAnchor: "start", dx: 6, fill: "currentColor"}))
  ]
})
TipWhat this shows · P4

Milan has about twice its “fair share” of knowledge jobs, but its home market ratio barely moved (2.20 in 2008, 2.19 in 2023), and it slips slightly without finance. Knowledge jobs are not concentrating in Milan faster than people. → Against A.

TipWhat this shows · T2

Milan’s graduate concentration ratio did not rise (1.36 in 2001, 1.32 in 2021). Graduates, the mobile factor in the model, are no more concentrated in Milan than twenty years ago, while Genoa’s ratio falls the most (1.33 → 1.15). → Against A.

T3 · Do young graduates leave Turin?

viewof yearM = Inputs.select(["Average 2019–2023", "2019", "2020", "2021", "2022", "2023"], {label: "Year"})
mobData = {
  const pick = c => {
    const rows = mobility.filter(d => d.city === c && (yearM === "Average 2019–2023" || d.year === +yearM));
    return d3.mean(rows, d => d.net_per_1000);
  };
  return {cities: cities.map(c => ({city: c, value: pick(c)})), north: pick("Northern Italy")};
}
Plot.plot({
  width,  // the page width, so text stays readable on phones
  height: 380, marginLeft: 110, marginRight: 50,
  x: {label: "net graduates gained (+) or lost (−) per 1,000 resident graduates →", grid: true},
  y: {label: null, domain: cities},
  marks: [
    Plot.barX(mobData.cities, {x: "value", y: "city", fill: d => colourOf[d.city],
      tip: {format: {x: "+.1f", fill: false}}}),
    Plot.text(mobData.cities, {x: d => Math.max(d.value, 0), y: "city", text: d => pct(d.value),
      textAnchor: "start", dx: 5, fill: "currentColor"}),
    Plot.ruleX([0]),
    Plot.ruleX([mobData.north], {strokeDasharray: "4 3", strokeOpacity: 0.6}),
    Plot.text([`northern Italy ${pct(mobData.north)}`], {x: mobData.north, frameAnchor: "top",
      textAnchor: "start", dx: 4, dy: -2, fill: "currentColor", fontSize: 11})
  ]
})

Italian graduates aged 25–39; includes moves within Italy and abroad; 2023 provisional. Source: ISTAT, Bes dei territori 2025 (indicator 11RIC025).

TipWhat this shows · T3

Bologna attracts more young graduates relative to its graduate population than Milan does (+40 against +36 per 1,000). Turin also gains young graduates on net (+14 per 1,000, above the northern average of +9.5). → Against A and C: there is no graduate outflow from Turin, so the “brain drain to Milan” mechanism is not visible.

TipWhat this shows · T8

Every big university city produces more graduates than it keeps as residents: Turin keeps 0.60 of its “fair share”, Milan 0.66, Bologna 0.43 and Padua 0.45. So Turin does not stand out from Milan here. → The “Turin trains, Milan employs” idea is not supported as something specific to Turin.

T9 · Around Milan: a place to live, not to work

Select Monza-Brianza, Bergamo and Brescia in the first chart above, with the measure set to Population and then to Knowledge jobs.

TipWhat this shows · T9

Where Milan does reshape its surroundings, it does so as a place to live rather than to work. Monza, Bergamo and Brescia gained population share but lost 6–8% of their knowledge-job share. This is consistent with Milan’s housing costs pushing residents outwards while the jobs stay in the city, although housing costs were not measured here. → Partly A: Milan’s core spreads as a place to live, not to work.
